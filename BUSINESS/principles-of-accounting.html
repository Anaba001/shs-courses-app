WHY DO YOU WANT TO STUDY PRINCIPLES OF ACCOUNTING?

Principles of accounting teach how business transactions are recorded, interpreted, and reported accurately.

Introduction to Principles of Accounting

Principles of accounting are the basic rules and concepts that guide the recording and reporting of financial information. They provide a framework for keeping accurate books of account and producing reliable financial statements. Accounting is important because every business needs to know how much it owns, owes, earns, and spends. These principles make sure that financial information is prepared consistently and understood by users such as owners, managers, investors, and creditors.

The accounting process begins with identifying financial transactions and then recording them in a systematic way. These records are then summarized and reported in statements that reveal the performance and financial position of a business. The principles of accounting help maintain order, fairness, and transparency in financial reporting.



Formula: Net Profit = Total Revenue - Total Expenses
Accounting principles turn everyday business transactions into clear and trustworthy financial information.

Accounting overview

Accounting Principles

Accounting principles are the foundation of the accounting profession. They guide how transactions should be recognized and measured. Some of the most important principles are the business entity concept, going concern concept, accrual basis, consistency concept, and matching concept. These principles give accountants a shared method for preparing reports and help users compare results from different organizations and periods.

They also support the reliability of financial statements because they reduce guesswork and ensure that financial records are prepared according to recognized standards. Understanding these principles is essential for anyone learning accounting.



Accounting principles create a common language for recording and reporting business events.

Principles note

Business entity

The business entity concept states that the business is treated as separate from its owner. This means the owner’s personal affairs should not be mixed with the business records. The business is seen as an independent unit for accounting purposes. This principle makes it possible to assess the performance of the business alone.

For example, if a business owner withdraws cash for personal use, that amount is recorded as drawings and not as an expense of the business. This keeps the accounting records accurate and clear.

The business entity principle keeps personal and business finances separate.

Business entity explanation

Going concern

The going concern concept assumes that the business will continue operating in the foreseeable future. This means financial statements are prepared on the assumption that the business will not be liquidated soon. As a result, assets are usually recorded at cost rather than at their sale value in a forced liquidation.

This principle is important because it affects the valuation of assets and liabilities and the way long-term financial statements are prepared.

Going concern assumes that the business will continue to operate unless there is evidence otherwise.

Going concern note

Accrual basis

The accrual basis of accounting records income when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, regardless of when cash is received or paid. This principle gives a more accurate view of the financial performance of a business. It prevents the accounts from being distorted by timing differences in cash flows.

For example, a business that sells goods on credit records the revenue immediately even if payment will be collected later. This makes the financial statements more useful for decision-making.

The accrual basis matches income and expenses to the period in which they arise.

Accrual concept note

Consistency

The consistency principle requires that the same accounting methods be applied from one period to another unless there is a good reason to change them. This allows financial statements to be compared meaningfully over time. If a business changes its method of depreciation or stock valuation, the change must be disclosed.

Consistency improves reliability and helps users understand whether changes in results are due to real performance or mere accounting changes.

Consistency ensures that accounting reports remain comparable over time.

Consistency principle note

Accounting Equation

The accounting equation is the foundation of double-entry accounting. It states that assets equal liabilities plus capital. This equation must always remain balanced after every transaction. It shows that the resources of the business are financed by either borrowings or the owner’s investment.

The equation is essential because it provides the structure for the balance sheet and keeps the records in balance. Every accounting transaction affects the equation in a way that preserves equality.



Formula: Assets = Liabilities + Capital
The accounting equation is the balancing rule that keeps all records consistent.

Equation note

Assets

Assets are resources owned by the business that are expected to bring future economic benefit. Examples include cash, buildings, machinery, inventory, and accounts receivable. Assets are classified as current or non-current depending on how quickly they can be converted into cash or used up.

Assets are important because they show the productive capacity and financial strength of the business.

Assets are the valuable resources controlled by the business.

Assets explanation

Liabilities

Liabilities are amounts owed by the business to outside parties. They include bank loans, trade payables, and other obligations. Liabilities show what the business owes and are important for assessing solvency and risk.

Liabilities represent the business obligations to external parties.

Liabilities note

Capital

Capital is the owner’s investment in the business. It may be increased by further contributions and profits, and reduced by drawings and losses. Capital is the residual interest in the assets after deducting liabilities.

Formula: Capital = Assets - Liabilities
Capital shows the owner’s stake in the business after all obligations are considered.

Capital note

Revenue and expenses

Revenue is the income earned by the business from its ordinary activities, such as sales of goods or services. Expenses are the costs incurred in earning that revenue. The difference between revenue and expenses determines profit or loss.

Understanding revenue and expenses is essential because they show whether the business is performing well or not.

Formula: Profit = Revenue - Expenses
Revenue and expenses determine whether a business earns a profit or suffers a loss.

Revenue and expense note

Books of Account

Books of account are the records used to keep financial information. They include the journal, ledger, cash book, and other subsidiary books. These records help businesses track transactions systematically and prepare financial statements accurately.

A well-kept set of books is essential for control, accountability, and evidence in case of disputes or audits.



Books of account organize transaction records into a useful system of financial reporting.

Books of account note

Journal

The journal is the first book of account where transactions are recorded in chronological order. Each entry includes the date, accounts affected, amounts, and a narration. The journal provides a complete record of events and is the basis for later posting to the ledger.

The journal records transactions in the order they occur.

Journal note

Ledger

The ledger is the book of final entry where transactions are grouped by account. It shows the balances of individual accounts such as cash, sales, purchases, and rent. The ledger is used to prepare the trial balance and the financial statements.

The ledger organizes transactions into separate accounts to show balances clearly.

Ledger note

Trial balance

The trial balance is a list of all ledger balances at a particular date. It is prepared to verify that total debits equal total credits. If the totals do not agree, the accountant checks the books for errors before preparing the final statements.

The trial balance checks whether the ledger is mathematically balanced.

Trial balance note

Cash book

The cash book records all cash and bank transactions. It acts like both a journal and a ledger and helps management monitor liquidity. A cash book is useful because it shows the cash position of the business at any time.

The cash book records all cash receipts and payments in one place.

Cash book note

Final Accounts

Final accounts are the statements prepared at the end of an accounting period to determine profitability and financial position. They include the trading account, profit and loss account, and balance sheet. These statements summarize the business results in a formal format for users.

Final accounts give a complete picture of what happened during the accounting period and how the business stands at the end of it.



Final accounts summarize the business performance and financial position at the end of the period.

Final accounts note

Trading account

The trading account shows the gross profit or gross loss of the business. It includes sales, opening stock, purchases, and closing stock. It helps show how much profit was made from buying and selling before overheads are considered.

Formula: Gross Profit = Sales - Cost of Goods Sold
The trading account measures profit from trading activities before operating expenses.

Trading account note

Profit and loss account

The profit and loss account calculates the net profit or net loss after all expenses and incomes have been considered. It begins with the gross profit from the trading account and then subtracts operating expenses to determine the net result.

Formula: Net Profit = Gross Profit - Operating Expenses
The profit and loss account shows the final operating result of the business.

Profit and loss note

Balance sheet

The balance sheet is a statement of financial position. It lists the assets, liabilities, and capital of the business at a particular date. It shows what the business owns, owes, and is worth.

Formula: Balance Sheet Equation = Assets - Liabilities = Capital
The balance sheet gives a snapshot of the financial position of the business.

Balance sheet note

Adjustments

Adjustments are entries made at the end of an accounting period to recognize revenue and expenses that relate to that period but have not yet been recorded. Examples include depreciation, accruals, prepayments, and bad debts. Adjustments ensure the financial statements are accurate and fair.

Formula: Adjusted Profit = Profit Before Adjustment + or - Adjustments
Adjustments ensure that financial statements reflect the true position of the business.

Adjustment note

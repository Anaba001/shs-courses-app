WHY DO YOU WANT TO STUDY CHEMISTRY?

Chemistry is the central science that explains the nature, structure, and transformations of matter. It gives meaning to the world around us through the study of atoms, compounds, and reactions. Learning chemistry helps you understand everyday processes such as cooking, cleaning, energy production, and the medicines that keep us healthy.

SENIOR HIGH SCHOOL CHEMISTRY

Chemistry is the science of matter, energy, and the changes that occur when substances interact with one another. It bridges the macroscopic world we observe with the atomic and molecular world that explains the behavior of all matter.

Senior High School Chemistry builds upon foundational concepts to provide students with a comprehensive understanding of chemical principles. This course explores atomic structure, chemical bonding, reactions, and organic chemistry. Students develop practical laboratory skills, learn to balance equations, and understand the fundamental laws that govern chemical interactions. Chemistry is essential for understanding processes in biology, physics, environmental science, and engineering.

Atomic Structure

Atomic structure is the foundation of chemistry. Understanding atoms—the basic units of matter—helps explain why elements behave as they do and how they combine to form compounds. The atom itself consists of smaller subatomic particles arranged in a specific manner.

Atoms and Elements

Atoms are the fundamental building blocks of all matter in the universe. Each element has a unique number of protons, which defines its atomic number and determines its position on the periodic table. For example, hydrogen has one proton, carbon has six protons, and oxygen has eight protons. Elements are named and organized based on their atomic properties and their position on the periodic table. The smallest representations of elements are individual atoms, while larger quantities are measured in moles. Understanding atoms is crucial for predicting how elements will react and combine. Atoms are extremely small—approximately 0.1 nanometers in diameter. Scientists use symbols to represent elements, such as H for hydrogen, C for carbon, and O for oxygen. These symbols appear on the periodic table and in chemical formulas. Every element has distinct characteristics including atomic mass, ionization energy, and electronegativity. The study of atomic structure has evolved significantly over centuries, from ancient philosophies to modern quantum mechanics.

An atom is the smallest unit of an element that retains the chemical properties of that element. Elements are pure substances composed of only one type of atom.

Subatomic Particles

Protons are positively charged particles located in the nucleus of an atom. The number of protons defines the atomic number and determines which element an atom represents. Neutrons are neutral particles also found in the nucleus alongside protons. They contribute to the atomic mass but do not affect the charge. Electrons are negatively charged particles that orbit the nucleus in electron shells or orbitals. The number of electrons typically equals the number of protons in a neutral atom. Protons have approximately 1,836 times the mass of an electron. Neutrons have a mass nearly equal to protons. The nucleus contains most of the atom's mass, yet it occupies only a tiny fraction of the atom's volume. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons, resulting in different atomic masses but identical chemical properties. The charge of a proton is +1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, while an electron has a charge of -1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs. Understanding subatomic particles explains why atoms are electrically neutral when they contain equal numbers of protons and electrons.

Subatomic particles are the smaller components that make up atoms: protons, neutrons, and electrons. Each plays a crucial role in determining an atom's identity and chemical behavior.

Atomic Number (Z) = Number of Protons
Mass Number (A) = Protons + Neutrons
Number of Electrons = Protons (in neutral atoms)

Electron Configuration

Electrons occupy orbitals arranged in shells or energy levels around the nucleus. The first shell can hold a maximum of 2 electrons, the second shell can hold 8 electrons, and the third shell can hold 18 electrons. Electrons fill orbitals from lowest energy to highest energy following the Aufbau principle. The s orbital is spherical, p orbitals are dumbbell-shaped, d orbitals are more complex, and f orbitals are even more intricate. Each orbital can contain a maximum of 2 electrons with opposite spins. The electron configuration can be written in several ways: full notation, noble gas notation, or orbital diagram. For example, oxygen has the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁴. Valence electrons are those in the outermost shell and determine the chemical reactivity of an element. Atoms tend to lose, gain, or share electrons to achieve a stable electron configuration, often resembling that of a noble gas. The periodic table is organized to reflect electron configurations—groups have the same number of valence electrons. Understanding electron configuration explains why elements in the same group have similar chemical properties.

Electron configuration describes the arrangement of electrons in orbitals around an atom's nucleus. It determines an atom's chemical properties and reactivity.

Orbital Filling Order (Aufbau Principle):
1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p...

Bohr Model of Atom

Niels Bohr proposed his atomic model in 1913 to explain how electrons behave in atoms. According to the Bohr model, electrons orbit the nucleus in discrete energy levels or shells. Each shell corresponds to a specific energy, and electrons can jump between levels by absorbing or emitting energy in the form of light. When an electron absorbs energy, it jumps to a higher energy level (excitation). When it returns to a lower level, it releases energy as light (emission). The energy of emitted light corresponds to the difference between energy levels. The Bohr model successfully predicted the hydrogen atom's spectrum. This model introduced the concept of quantized energy levels, meaning electrons can only have specific energy values. The model works exceptionally well for hydrogen but is less accurate for larger atoms with multiple electrons. Modern quantum mechanics has replaced the Bohr model with more sophisticated orbital theory. However, the Bohr model remains useful for understanding basic atomic structure and energy transitions. The model predicts that atoms emit characteristic spectral lines when excited electrons return to lower energy levels.

The Bohr model represents the atom as a nucleus surrounded by electrons in fixed, circular orbits at specific energy levels, similar to planets orbiting the sun.

Energy of electron transition: ΔE = E_final - E_initial = hf
where h = Planck's constant (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)
f = frequency of emitted light

Periodic Table and Trends

The periodic table is one of the most important tools in chemistry, organizing all known elements according to their properties and electron configurations. Understanding the periodic table helps predict how elements will behave and react.

Organization of Periodic Table

The periodic table contains 118 known elements arranged to show patterns in their properties. Elements are organized by increasing atomic number from left to right and top to bottom. Horizontal rows are called periods, and there are 7 periods in the periodic table. Vertical columns are called groups or families, and there are 18 groups. Elements in the same group share similar chemical properties because they have the same number of valence electrons. The periodic table is divided into blocks: s-block, p-block, d-block, and f-block, referring to the type of orbital being filled. The main group elements occupy groups 1, 2, and 13-18 and include metals, nonmetals, and metalloids. Transition metals occupy groups 3-12 in the d-block. Lanthanides and actinides are the f-block elements placed separately below the main table. The periodic table also contains natural groupings: alkali metals (group 1), alkaline earth metals (group 2), halogens (group 17), and noble gases (group 18). Each element's position reveals important information about its atomic structure and chemical behavior. Scientists continue to synthesize new elements and add them to the periodic table.

The periodic table arranges elements in rows (periods) and columns (groups) based on their atomic number and electron configuration, revealing patterns in their chemical behavior.

Periodic Trends

Atomic radius increases as you move down a group because new electron shells are added. Atomic radius decreases as you move across a period because electron shells are filling with electrons closer to the nucleus. Ionization energy is the energy required to remove an electron from an atom. It increases across a period and decreases down a group. Elements with higher ionization energies hold their electrons more tightly and are less likely to form positive ions. Electronegativity measures an atom's tendency to attract electrons in a chemical bond. It increases across a period and decreases down a group. Nonmetals have higher electronegativity values, while metals have lower values. Electron affinity is the energy change when an electron is added to an atom. Atomic mass increases moving down the periodic table and generally increases across periods. Metallic character (the tendency to lose electrons and form positive ions) decreases across a period and increases down a group. Nonmetallic character shows the opposite trend. These trends are essential for predicting how elements will interact and bond with each other.

Periodic trends are recurring patterns in element properties that change predictably as you move across periods or down groups in the periodic table.

Electronegativity difference (ΔEN) determines bond type:
ΔEN < 0.5: Nonpolar covalent
0.5 ≤ ΔEN ≤ 1.7: Polar covalent
ΔEN > 1.7: Ionic

Groups and Periods

Group 1 contains the alkali metals, which are highly reactive metals that readily lose one electron to form +1 ions. Examples include lithium (Li), sodium (Na), and potassium (K). Group 2 contains the alkaline earth metals, which are reactive metals that lose two electrons to form +2 ions. Examples include magnesium (Mg) and calcium (Ca). Group 13 through 18 are p-block elements that gain electrons to achieve noble gas configuration. Group 13 elements like aluminum (Al) typically form +3 ions. Halogens (Group 17) are highly reactive nonmetals that readily gain one electron to form -1 ions. Examples include fluorine (F), chlorine (Cl), and bromine (Br). Noble gases (Group 18) are unreactive elements with complete valence shells. Examples include helium (He), neon (Ne), and argon (Ar). Transition metals in groups 3-12 can form multiple oxidation states and are often used as catalysts. The lanthanides and actinides are placed separately and are called rare earth elements. Period 1 contains only hydrogen and helium. Period 2 contains lithium through neon with 2 electron shells. Each subsequent period adds one more electron shell, explaining the increase in atomic size down a group.

Groups (vertical columns) contain elements with similar electron configurations and chemical properties, while periods (horizontal rows) represent the number of electron shells.

Chemical Bonding

Chemical bonding explains how atoms combine to form compounds. The type of bonding between atoms determines the properties of the resulting substance.

Ionic Bonding

Ionic bonds form between atoms with large differences in electronegativity, typically between metals (which lose electrons) and nonmetals (which gain electrons). In ionic bonding, electrons are transferred completely from one atom to another. The atom losing electrons becomes a positively charged cation, while the atom gaining electrons becomes a negatively charged anion. Ionic compounds form a three-dimensional crystalline lattice structure where cations and anions alternate in an organized pattern. Electrostatic forces hold the ions together in an ionic crystal. Common examples of ionic compounds include sodium chloride (NaCl), magnesium oxide (MgO), and calcium carbonate (CaCO₃). Ionic compounds typically have high melting and boiling points because breaking the ionic bonds requires significant energy. Ionic compounds dissolve in polar solvents like water, where the solvent molecules surround and separate the ions. When dissolved in water or melted, ionic compounds conduct electricity because the ions are free to move. The arrangement of ions in an ionic crystal depends on the sizes and charges of the ions involved. Ionic compounds are usually hard and brittle, shattering easily under stress. Understanding ionic bonding explains why metals and nonmetals react so readily to form stable compounds.

Ionic bonding occurs when electrons are transferred from one atom to another, forming positively charged cations and negatively charged anions that attract each other electrostatically.

Example: Sodium (Na) + Chlorine (Cl) → Sodium Chloride (NaCl)
Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
Na⁺ and Cl⁻ attract to form NaCl

Covalent Bonding

Covalent bonds form between atoms with similar electronegativities, typically between nonmetals. In covalent bonding, electron pairs are shared between atoms. A single covalent bond involves the sharing of one pair of electrons between two atoms. A double covalent bond involves the sharing of two pairs of electrons. A triple covalent bond involves the sharing of three pairs of electrons. Single bonds are weaker and longer than double bonds. Double bonds are stronger and shorter than triple bonds. Covalent molecules are often represented using Lewis structures, which show valence electrons as dots or lines. The shared electrons reside in molecular orbitals between the nuclei. Covalent compounds typically have low melting and boiling points compared to ionic compounds. Many covalent compounds are gases or liquids at room temperature. Covalent compounds are usually soluble in nonpolar solvents like carbon tetrachloride but insoluble in water. Examples of covalent compounds include methane (CH₄), oxygen gas (O₂), and water (H₂O). Polar covalent bonds form when atoms have different electronegativities, causing unequal electron sharing. Nonpolar covalent bonds form when atoms have identical or very similar electronegativities, resulting in equal electron sharing.

Covalent bonding occurs when atoms share electrons to achieve stability, with the shared electrons orbiting both nuclei.

Example: Hydrogen gas (H₂)
H• + •H → H:H or H-H
Example: Oxygen gas (O₂)
O::O or O=O (double bond)

Metallic Bonding

Metallic bonding occurs in metals and alloys where valence electrons are delocalized and mobile. Unlike ionic bonding, electrons are not transferred to specific atoms. Unlike covalent bonding, electrons are not localized between two atoms. Instead, valence electrons form a "sea" that flows throughout the entire metal structure. Metal cations occupy fixed positions in a crystal lattice while electrons move freely. This electron sea model explains many properties of metals. The mobility of electrons allows metals to conduct electricity in solid form, unlike ionic compounds which only conduct when molten or dissolved. The delocalized electrons can absorb and release light, giving metals their characteristic shine and luster. Metallic bonding explains why metals are malleable (can be hammered into sheets) and ductile (can be drawn into wires). Metals have high melting and boiling points due to the strength of metallic bonding. The strength of metallic bonding increases with the number of valence electrons and the size of the metal. Alloys are mixtures of two or more metals that exhibit metallic bonding. Examples include brass (copper and zinc) and steel (iron and carbon). Metallic bonding creates the characteristic properties that make metals valuable for construction, electronics, and manufacturing.

Metallic bonding is the attraction between metal atoms and a shared "sea" of delocalized electrons that move freely throughout the metal structure.

Metallic structure: Metal cations + delocalized electron sea
Example: Copper (Cu) → Cu⁺ + e⁻
The electrons form a mobile "sea" throughout the structure

States of Matter

Matter exists in different physical states: solid, liquid, and gas. The state of matter depends on temperature, pressure, and the strength of intermolecular forces.

Solid State

In solids, particles (atoms, ions, or molecules) are held in fixed positions by strong intermolecular or ionic forces. Solids have a definite shape that does not change unless an external force is applied. Solids have a definite volume that remains constant. Particles in solids vibrate in place but cannot move freely from position to position. The vibration increases with temperature, eventually causing the solid to melt. There are two main types of solids: crystalline and amorphous. Crystalline solids have a regular, repeating pattern of particles arranged in a crystal lattice. Amorphous solids lack long-range order and appear similar to liquids but maintain a rigid structure. Diamond is a crystalline solid with an extremely strong structure due to covalent bonding between carbon atoms. Metals are crystalline solids with metallic bonding. Ice is a crystalline solid formed when water freezes. Glass is an amorphous solid that appears solid but lacks the ordered structure of crystals. Solids are generally incompressible because there is little space between particles. Most solids are denser than their liquid counterparts because particles are packed more tightly. The properties of a solid depend on the type of bonding and the arrangement of particles.

Solids are rigid substances with definite shape and volume, where particles are tightly packed and held in fixed positions by strong intermolecular forces.

Liquid State

In liquids, particles move more freely than in solids but remain closely packed. Liquids have a definite volume that does not change unless heated or cooled. Liquids lack a definite shape and conform to the shape of their container. Surface tension in liquids arises from the cohesive forces between particles, causing the liquid surface to behave like a thin elastic sheet. Viscosity measures a liquid's resistance to flow—honey has higher viscosity than water. Some liquids like water show capillary action, where the liquid climbs up a narrow tube due to intermolecular adhesive forces. Liquids are typically difficult to compress because particles are already close together. The boiling point of a liquid depends on intermolecular forces—stronger forces require higher temperatures to escape into the gas phase. Evaporation occurs when particles at the surface of a liquid gain sufficient energy to escape into the gas phase. Condensation is the reverse process where gas particles lose energy and form liquid. The transition between solid and liquid occurs at the melting point. Water is the most common liquid on Earth and has unusual properties like expansion upon freezing. Liquid water is denser than solid ice, explaining why ice floats on water. Liquids play a crucial role in biological systems, chemical reactions, and industrial processes.

Liquids are substances with definite volume but indefinite shape, where particles have more freedom of movement than in solids but remain close together through intermolecular forces.

Surface Tension (γ): Force per unit length (N/m or dyne/cm)
Viscosity (η): Measured in Pascal-seconds (Pa·s) or poise
Example: Water at 25°C has η ≈ 0.001 Pa·s

Gaseous State

In gases, particles move rapidly and randomly in straight lines until they collide. Gases expand to fill the entire volume of their container. Gases lack a definite shape and adopt the shape of their container. Intermolecular forces in gases are negligible compared to liquids and solids. Gas particles are far apart and have significant empty space between them. The kinetic molecular theory explains gas behavior: particles move in random motion, collisions are elastic, and average kinetic energy is proportional to absolute temperature. Pressure results from gas particles colliding with container walls. Boyle's Law states that pressure and volume are inversely proportional at constant temperature: PV = k. Charles's Law states that volume and absolute temperature are directly proportional at constant pressure: V/T = k. Gay-Lussac's Law states that pressure and absolute temperature are directly proportional at constant volume: P/T = k. The ideal gas law combines these relationships: PV = nRT, where n is moles and R is the gas constant. Real gases deviate from ideal behavior at high pressures and low temperatures. Diffusion is the spreading of gas particles throughout a space. Effusion is the escape of gas molecules through a small opening. Graham's Law relates the rates of effusion or diffusion to molar masses of gases.

Gases are substances with no definite shape or volume, where particles move rapidly in straight lines and are far apart, interacting only through collisions.

Ideal Gas Law: PV = nRT
where P = pressure (Pa), V = volume (m³), n = moles (mol)
R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), T = absolute temperature (K)
Boyle's Law: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ (at constant T)
Charles's Law: V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ (at constant P)

Phase Transitions

Melting is the transition from solid to liquid, occurring at the melting point when thermal energy overcomes intermolecular forces. Freezing is the reverse of melting, where liquid becomes solid. Evaporation is the transition from liquid to gas, where particles at the surface escape into the gas phase. Condensation is the reverse of evaporation, where gas particles lose energy and become liquid. Sublimation is the transition directly from solid to gas without passing through the liquid phase. Deposition is the reverse of sublimation, where gas becomes solid. Dry ice (solid carbon dioxide) undergoes sublimation at room temperature and atmospheric pressure. Each phase transition requires a specific amount of heat energy called latent heat. Latent heat of fusion is the energy required to melt a solid or released during freezing. Latent heat of vaporization is the energy required to evaporate a liquid or released during condensation. Water's latent heat of vaporization (40.66 kJ/mol) is exceptionally high, requiring significant energy to convert liquid to steam. During phase transitions, temperature remains constant despite heat absorption or release because energy goes into breaking bonds rather than increasing particle motion. A phase diagram graphically shows which phase is stable at different temperatures and pressures. The triple point is the unique temperature and pressure where all three phases coexist in equilibrium. The critical point is the highest temperature and pressure at which distinct phases can exist.

Phase transitions are changes between states of matter (solid ↔ liquid ↔ gas) that occur when substances are heated or cooled.

Heat required for phase transition: Q = m × L
where m = mass (kg), L = latent heat (J/kg)
For water: Latent heat of fusion ≈ 334 kJ/kg
Latent heat of vaporization ≈ 2,260 kJ/kg

Chemical Reactions and Equations

Chemical reactions are processes where reactants are transformed into products through breaking and forming of chemical bonds. Balanced equations represent these reactions with proper stoichiometry.

Types of Reactions

Combination (synthesis) reactions involve two or more reactants joining to form a single product. Example: 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl. Decomposition reactions involve a single reactant breaking down into two or more products. Example: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂. Single displacement reactions occur when a more reactive element replaces a less reactive element in a compound. Example: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂. Double displacement (precipitation) reactions involve the exchange of ions between two compounds. Example: AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃. Combustion reactions involve a substance reacting with oxygen, typically releasing energy in the form of heat and light. Example: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Acid-base reactions involve the neutralization of acids and bases. Example: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. Redox (oxidation-reduction) reactions involve transfer of electrons between reactants. Precipitation reactions form an insoluble solid (precipitate) in solution. Neutralization reactions produce water and a salt from acid and base. Combustion reactions are always exothermic (release heat). Chain reactions, including free-radical and nuclear chain reactions, involve sequential reactions that propagate rapidly. Understanding reaction types helps predict products and balance equations.

Chemical reactions are classified into main types based on what happens to reactants: combination, decomposition, displacement, and combustion reactions.

Balancing Equations

present reactions with reactants on the left and products on the right. Coefficients are numbers placed before formulas to balance equations. Subscripts are numbers within formulas that indicate how many atoms are in a compound and should NOT be changed. Start balancing by counting atoms of each element on both sides. Balance one element at a time, working systematically through all elements. It is usually easier to balance metals first, then nonmetals, and finally hydrogen and oxygen. If oxygen appears on both sides, balance it last. Common strategies include doubling all coefficients if needed or adjusting one coefficient at a time. Check your work by verifying that each element has equal atoms on both sides. The smallest whole-number coefficients produce the simplest balanced equation. Sometimes it is necessary to use fractional coefficients, but typically we multiply all coefficients to achieve whole numbers. For example: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl balances with coefficient 1 for H₂, 1 for Cl₂, and 2 for HCl. State symbols show the physical state: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous. Ionic equations can show soluble salts as separate ions in solution. Net ionic equations show only species that actually participate in the reaction.

Balancing chemical equations ensures that the number of atoms of each element is equal on both sides, satisfying the law of conservation of mass.

Example: Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃
Start: Fe (1), O (2) on left; Fe (2), O (3) on right
Balance: 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃
Check: Fe (4), O (6) on both sides ✓

Stoichiometry

Stoichiometry uses balanced equations to calculate the amounts of substances involved in reactions. Mole ratios from balanced equations indicate the relative quantities of reactants and products. The mole is the standard unit of amount of substance in chemistry, defined as 6.022 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's number). Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance, typically expressed in g/mol. Conversions between mass and moles use the formula: moles = mass / molar mass. Conversions between moles of different substances use mole ratios from balanced equations. Limiting reactants are reactants completely consumed in a reaction, limiting the amount of product formed. Excess reactants remain after the reaction ends. Theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product that could form from given amounts of reactants. Actual yield is the amount of product actually obtained in an experiment. Percent yield compares actual yield to theoretical yield: (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100%. In a stoichiometric calculation: (1) balance the equation, (2) convert known quantities to moles, (3) use mole ratios to find moles of unknown, (4) convert moles to desired units. Stoichiometric ratios allow calculation of products from known reactant quantities. This helps chemists plan reactions, predict yields, and control processes efficiently.

Stoichiometry is the study of quantitative relationships in chemical reactions, allowing calculation of amounts of reactants and products.

Example: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
Mole ratio: 2 mol H₂ : 1 mol O₂ : 2 mol H₂O
If 4 moles of H₂ react: 4 mol H₂ × (2 mol H₂O / 2 mol H₂) = 4 mol H₂O
Percent Yield = (Actual Yield / Theoretical Yield) × 100%

Acids, Bases and pH

Acids and bases are important chemical classifications with distinct properties. The pH scale quantifies acidity and basicity of solutions.

Acid Definition and Properties

Acids are compounds that increase the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺) in aqueous solution. The Arrhenius definition states that acids dissociate to produce H⁺ ions. The Brønsted-Lowry definition states that acids are proton donors. Strong acids completely ionize in water, including HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HBr, HI, and HClO₄. Weak acids partially ionize in water and exist in equilibrium: HA ↔ H⁺ + A⁻. Examples of weak acids include acetic acid (CH₃COOH), carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), and hydrofluoric acid (HF). Diprotic acids like sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) can donate two protons. Triprotic acids like phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) can donate three protons. Monoprotic acids like hydrochloric acid (HCl) donate one proton. Acids taste sour, though tasting unknown substances is dangerous in laboratories. Acids turn blue litmus paper red. Acids react with many metals, producing hydrogen gas. Acids react with carbonates and bicarbonates, producing carbon dioxide gas. Acids have pH values below 7 in aqueous solution. Strong acids have higher conductivity in solution than weak acids. Concentrated acids are dangerous and can cause severe burns.

Acids are substances that donate protons (H⁺ ions) in water according to Brønsted-Lowry theory, or substances that form H⁺ ions according to Arrhenius theory.

Strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄
Weak acid example: CH₃COOH ↔ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺
Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]

Base Definition and Properties

Bases are compounds that decrease the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺) in aqueous solution. The Arrhenius definition states that bases dissociate to produce hydroxide ions (OH⁻). The Brønsted-Lowry definition states that bases are proton acceptors. Strong bases completely dissociate in water, including NaOH, KOH, LiOH, Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, and Ba(OH)₂. Weak bases partially dissociate in water and exist in equilibrium: B + H₂O ↔ BH⁺ + OH⁻. Ammonia (NH₃) is a common weak base. Amines (compounds containing nitrogen bonded to carbon) are also weak bases. Bases taste bitter, though tasting unknown substances is dangerous. Bases feel slippery on skin and can cause chemical burns. Bases turn red litmus paper blue. Bases react with acids in neutralization reactions. Bases have pH values above 7 in aqueous solution. Strong bases have higher conductivity than weak bases. Bases are used in many industrial processes, household cleaners, and biological systems. Antacids like calcium hydroxide or magnesium hydroxide neutralize stomach acid. The pH of a basic solution depends on the concentration and strength of the base.

Bases are substances that accept protons (H⁺ ions) according to Brønsted-Lowry theory, or substances that form OH⁻ ions in water according to Arrhenius theory.

Strong bases: NaOH, KOH, LiOH, Ba(OH)₂
Weak base example: NH₃ + H₂O ↔ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻
Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻]/[B]

pH Scale

pH is defined as -log[H⁺], where [H⁺] is the molar concentration of hydrogen ions. Pure water at 25°C has a pH of exactly 7.0, considered neutral. Acidic solutions have pH less than 7, with more H⁺ ions than OH⁻ ions. Basic (alkaline) solutions have pH greater than 7, with more OH⁻ ions than H⁺ ions. The pH scale is logarithmic: each decrease of one pH unit means the H⁺ concentration increases 10-fold. A solution with pH 3 is 10 times more acidic than one with pH 4. Battery acid has pH around 0-1, while lye solutions have pH around 13-14. Stomach acid (gastric juice) has pH around 1.5-3.5 due to hydrochloric acid. Neutral pure water has pH 7, but rainwater is slightly acidic (pH ~5-6) due to dissolved CO₂. Blood is slightly basic with pH around 7.4. Saliva has pH around 6.5-7.5. Soil can range from very acidic (pH 3-4) to very basic (pH 8-9). pH meters and pH paper are used to measure pH. Buffers maintain relatively constant pH even when acids or bases are added. The pOH scale measures hydroxide ion concentration: pOH = -log[OH⁻]. At 25°C: pH + pOH = 14.0.

The pH scale measures the concentration of hydrogen ions in a solution on a logarithmic scale from 0 to 14, where 7 is neutral, values below 7 are acidic, and values above 7 are basic.

pH = -log[H⁺]
[H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ
pOH = -log[OH⁻]
pH + pOH = 14 (at 25°C)
Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ (at 25°C)

Neutralization Reactions

Neutralization is a reaction between an acid and a base that produces water and a salt. The general form is: Acid + Base → Salt + Water. In ionic form: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. The net ionic equation for strong acid-strong base neutralization is always: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. Example: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. The neutralization of weak acids by strong bases produces salts that are basic (pH > 7) because the conjugate base is a weak base. The neutralization of strong acids by weak bases produces salts that are acidic (pH < 7) because the conjugate acid is a weak acid. Titration is an analytical technique using neutralization to determine the concentration of an acid or base. In titration, a solution of known concentration (standard solution) is added until the reaction is complete, indicated by a color change from an indicator. The equivalence point is where moles of acid equal moles of base. The endpoint is indicated by a color change of the indicator and should occur at or very near the equivalence point. Common indicators include phenolphthalein (colorless in acid, pink in base) and methyl orange (red in acid, yellow in base). The calculations use the relationship: (concentration₁)(volume₁) = (concentration₂)(volume₂) for equimolar reactions. Heat is released during neutralization (exothermic reaction). The temperature increase indicates the reaction's extent and releases approximately 57.3 kJ/mol of water formed.

Neutralization reactions occur when acids and bases react to form water and a salt, consuming H⁺ and OH⁻ ions.

HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O
H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
Titration: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ (for 1:1 ratio)
For H₂SO₄ and NaOH: M₁V₁ × 2 = M₂V₂

Organic Chemistry

Organic chemistry focuses on carbon-containing compounds, which are the basis for all living organisms and countless synthetic materials.

Hydrocarbons

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons containing only single bonds between carbon atoms. They follow the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Examples include methane (CH₄), ethane (C₂H₆), propane (C₃H₈), and butane (C₄H₁₀). Alkanes are nonpolar and relatively unreactive, making them useful as fuels and lubricants. Natural gas is primarily methane, while crude oil contains a mixture of alkanes. Branched alkanes (isomers) have the same molecular formula but different structures and properties. Alkenes contain at least one carbon-carbon double bond (C=C) and follow the general formula CₙH₂ₙ. Examples include ethene (C₂H₄), propene (C₃H₆), and butene (C₄H₈). The double bond makes alkenes more reactive than alkanes, readily undergoing addition reactions. Polyethylene is produced by polymerizing ethene into long chains. Alkynes contain at least one carbon-carbon triple bond (C≡C) and follow the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂. Examples include ethyne/acetylene (C₂H₂), propyne (C₃H₄), and butyne (C₄H₆). Alkynes are even more reactive than alkenes. Aromatic hydrocarbons contain benzene rings (C₆H₆) with alternating single and double bonds. Benzene and its derivatives are relatively stable and used in many industrial products. Combustion of hydrocarbons releases energy, making them important fuels.

Hydrocarbons are organic compounds containing only carbon and hydrogen atoms. Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons; alkenes contain double bonds; alkynes contain triple bonds.

Alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ → CH₄, C₂H₆, C₃H₈, C₄H₁₀
Alkenes: CₙH₂ₙ → C₂H₄, C₃H₆, C₄H₈
Alkynes: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ → C₂H₂, C₃H₄, C₄H₆
Benzene combustion: C₆H₆ + 7.5O₂ → 6CO₂ + 3H₂O

Functional Groups

Functional groups are collections of atoms with predictable chemical behavior. The hydroxyl group (-OH) defines alcohols, making them polar and water-soluble. Examples include methanol (CH₃OH) and ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH). The carbonyl group (C=O) defines aldehydes and ketones. Aldehydes have the carbonyl at the end of a chain; ketones have it in the middle. The carboxyl group (-COOH) defines carboxylic acids, which are weak acids. Acetic acid (CH₃COOH) is found in vinegar. Esters form when carboxylic acids react with alcohols, producing pleasant fragrances and flavors. The amine group (-NH₂) defines amines, which are weak bases. Amines are found in proteins and many pharmaceutical compounds. The ether group (R-O-R') is less reactive than alcohols. Amides (R-CO-NH₂) are formed when carboxylic acids react with amines and are important in proteins. The thiol group (-SH) is similar to alcohols but contains sulfur instead of oxygen. Disulfide bonds (-S-S-) link protein chains. Recognition of functional groups allows prediction of reactivity and properties. Different functional groups give molecules vastly different properties despite similar molecular formulas. Organic molecules often contain multiple functional groups.

Functional groups are characteristic groupings of atoms in organic molecules that determine the molecule's chemical properties and reactivity.

Common Functional Groups:
Alcohol: -OH
Aldehyde: -CHO
Ketone: -CO- (in chain)
Carboxylic acid: -COOH
Ester: -COO-
Amine: -NH₂
Ether: -O-
Thiol: -SH

Isomerism

Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different connectivity of atoms. Chain isomers differ in the carbon skeleton arrangement. For example, butane (CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃) and isobutane or 2-methylpropane [(CH₃)₂CHCH₃] are chain isomers. Position isomers have functional groups in different positions. For example, 1-propanol (CH₃CH₂CH₂OH) and 2-propanol [(CH₃)₂CHOH] are position isomers. Functional group isomers have different functional groups. For example, ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) and dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃) are functional group isomers. Geometric isomers (cis-trans isomers) result from restricted rotation around double bonds. Cis-isomers have groups on the same side; trans-isomers have them on opposite sides. Stereoisomers include geometric and optical isomers but have the same connectivity. Optical isomers (enantiomers) are mirror images of each other that cannot be superimposed. They differ in their interaction with polarized light and biological systems. A chiral center is a carbon bonded to four different groups, creating optical isomers. Isomers can have significantly different properties and biological activities despite identical molecular formulas. The presence of stereoisomers is important in pharmaceutical chemistry where one enantiomer might be active and the other inactive or harmful.

Isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural arrangements or spatial orientations of atoms.

Examples of Isomers:
Pentane (C₅H₁₂): n-pentane, isopentane, neopentane
Propanol (C₃H₈O): 1-propanol, 2-propanol
Dichloroethene (C₂H₂Cl₂): cis-1,2-dichloroethene, trans-1,2-dichloroethene

Redox Reactions

Redox reactions involve transfer of electrons between reactants, changing oxidation states of elements.

Oxidation and Reduction

Oxidation is defined as the loss of electrons from an atom, ion, or molecule. The oxidation state of an element increases during oxidation. An oxidizing agent is the substance that gets reduced (gains electrons) and causes oxidation of another substance. Reduction is the gain of electrons by an atom, ion, or molecule. The oxidation state of an element decreases during reduction. A reducing agent is the substance that gets oxidized (loses electrons) and causes reduction of another substance. In redox reactions, one reactant is oxidized (loses electrons) while another is reduced (gains electrons). The total number of electrons lost equals the total number of electrons gained. Redox reactions power batteries and fuel cells through controlled electron transfer. Photosynthesis is a redox process where water is oxidized and carbon dioxide is reduced. Cellular respiration involves oxidation of glucose to provide energy. Combustion reactions are typically redox reactions where fuel is oxidized and oxygen is reduced. Corrosion of metals involves oxidation, while rust formation on iron involves oxygen as the oxidizing agent. Electroplating uses redox reactions to coat one metal with another. Bleach works through oxidation of stains and pathogens. Half-reactions show either oxidation or reduction separately and are used to balance redox equations.

Oxidation is loss of electrons (or gain of oxygen); reduction is gain of electrons (or loss of oxygen). Redox reactions always involve both oxidation and reduction occurring simultaneously.

Example: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu
Oxidation: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻
Reduction: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
Zn is the reducing agent; Cu²⁺ is the oxidizing agent

Oxidation States

Oxidation states are assigned using a set of rules to track electron transfer in reactions. The oxidation state of an element in its elemental form is zero. The oxidation state of a monatomic ion equals the charge of the ion. Oxygen usually has an oxidation state of -2 (except in peroxides where it is -1). Hydrogen usually has an oxidation state of +1 (except in metal hydrides where it is -1). Group 1 metals always have +1 oxidation state in compounds. Group 2 metals always have +2 oxidation state in compounds. Halogens usually have -1 oxidation state (except in oxygen compounds). The sum of oxidation states in a neutral compound equals zero. The sum of oxidation states in a polyatomic ion equals the ion's charge. In MnO₄⁻: Mn + 4(-2) = -1, so Mn = +7. In K₂Cr₂O₇: 2(+1) + 2x + 7(-2) = 0, so Cr = +6. Oxidation state increases during oxidation and decreases during reduction. Identifying oxidation state changes helps balance redox equations. Elements that can have multiple oxidation states are often involved in redox reactions. Transition metals commonly form compounds with varying oxidation states.

Oxidation states are numbers assigned to elements in compounds that represent the number of electrons lost or gained by an atom in that compound.

Oxidation state assignment:
Fe₂O₃: Fe = +3, O = -2
NO₃⁻: N = +5, O = -2
H₂O₂: H = +1, O = -1
SO₄²⁻: S = +6, O = -2

Balancing Redox Equations

The half-reaction method is the most systematic approach for balancing redox equations. First, separate the equation into oxidation and reduction half-reactions. For each half-reaction: balance all atoms except oxygen and hydrogen, then balance oxygen using water, balance hydrogen using H⁺ (or OH⁻ in basic solution), and finally balance charge using electrons. Next, multiply each half-reaction by appropriate factors so that electrons gained equals electrons lost. Finally, add the balanced half-reactions and cancel species appearing on both sides. Example: Fe²⁺ + MnO₄⁻ → Fe³⁺ + Mn²⁺. Oxidation: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻. Reduction: MnO₄⁻ → Mn²⁺. Balance oxygen in reduction: MnO₄⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O. Add H⁺: 8H⁺ + MnO₄⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O. Balance charge: 8H⁺ + MnO₄⁻ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O. Multiply oxidation by 5: 5Fe²⁺ → 5Fe³⁺ + 5e⁻. Add: 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ + MnO₄⁻ → 5Fe³⁺ + Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O. In acidic solutions, use H⁺ to balance hydrogen. In basic solutions, use OH⁻ to balance hydrogen. The oxidation number method involves identifying elements that change oxidation state and using smallest whole number coefficients. Polyatomic ions often appear unchanged in the final equation. Practice with various redox systems improves balancing skills.

Balancing redox equations requires using the half-reaction method to balance both atoms and electrons in oxidation and reduction processes.

Half-reaction method steps:
1. Write half-reactions
2. Balance atoms (except O, H)
3. Balance O using H₂O
4. Balance H using H⁺ (or OH⁻)
5. Balance charge using e⁻
6. Equalize electrons between half-reactions
7. Add and simplify
